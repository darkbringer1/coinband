Is an AI forecaster a better risk model? Check it yourself.

Every hour from May to November 2026, Mistral's Timestral and GARCH, the standard statistical volatility model, each drew a line under the price: "the price should fall below this only 1 hour in 100" (or 1 in 20). GARCH was tuned on the six months before; Timestral was used as released. Scrub through the months below and count the breaches yourself. The full story is in part 2.

1. Risk lines, hour by hour

Each line is drawn one hour ahead from the prices known at that time; a dot marks an hour that closed below that model's line. A good risk model is crossed about as often as it promises: 1 in 100 hours (1%) or 1 in 20 (5%). Fewer crossings mean it is overcautious, more mean it underestimates risk. "Average miss" is the quantile loss: how far, on average, the price landed on the wrong side, weighted the way the target level implies. Lower is better.

2. The volatility race, day by day

Top: each day's actual volatility over the next 24 hours (annualized) and what each model expected at the start of the day. Bottom: the running average of each model's forecast error for volatility (QLIKE), so the lowest line is winning. The blend is the average of Timestral's and GARCH's forecast ranges.

3. And against the options market?

For Bitcoin and Ether we also compared Timestral with Deribit's DVOL, the volatility that option prices imply. Timestral forecast the next day's volatility better than DVOL and carried information DVOL had not priced in. Deribit's terms do not allow republishing its data, so that comparison lives in part 3 as summary statistics only.
